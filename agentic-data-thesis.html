Thesis

The central claim, in one paragraph

The whole thesis

Agents need structured, joinable, temporally-explicit, provenance-tracked facts — not blobs of text retrieved by cosine similarity. Naive vector RAG is empirically inferior on every dimension that matters for cross-document, multi-hop, temporal, audit-bound problems, but superior on simple fact retrieval. So the correct architecture is hybrid, with four pillars: (1) deterministic, schema-driven extraction with constrained decoding and span-grounded citations; (2) probabilistic entity resolution rooted in Fellegi–Sunter with calibrated posteriors; (3) a bitemporal knowledge graph carrying valid-time and system-time on every edge; (4) W3C PROV-extended provenance with content-addressable source storage and signed extraction artifacts. This spine is what an AI-native KYC entrant defends on. Screening lists are commodity; the inference chain from byte stream to decision is not.

The four pillars at a glance

Deterministic extraction
XGrammar / Outlines / llguidance + Pydantic + dual-LLM verification + span-grounded citations to {page, bbox, ocr_text}. Schema-first, not text-first.
II
Entity resolution
Splink (Fellegi–Sunter, EM-trained m/u weights, additive log-Bayes factors) on DuckDB / Spark / Postgres. Senzing or Quantexa only if you need their network overlays.
III
Bitemporal graph
Property graph with (start_valid, end_valid, start_system, end_system) on every edge. Graphiti for live agent memory; XTDB if you want pure bitemporal.
IV
Provenance
PROV-AGENT (W3C PROV + MCP) at the workflow level, span-grounded citations at the field level, signed extraction artifacts, immutable source bytes.

Why this matters for a founder

Existing KYC vendors expose opaque scores. Persona returns a verdict, ComplyAdvantage returns hits, Onfido returns a confidence — without exposing the inference chain. An AI-native entrant differentiates not on screening-list breadth (commoditized; in March 2026 Sumsub announced that ComplyAdvantage now powers its AML layer via API) but on the deterministic-extraction → ER → bitemporal-graph → provenance spine that makes every decision interpretable, replayable, and defensible. That is what a regulator pays for, and it is the moat the incumbents have not built.

Chapter 1 · Part I

Why naive RAG fails for agentic, audit-bound work

Evidenced ~50 min read

The single most important fact you will read this week is the empirical performance gap between vector-only retrieval and graph-augmented retrieval on the kinds of questions agents actually face in production. The literature in 2024–2025 has converged: on simple fact lookups, basic RAG is fine — and sometimes better. On multi-hop, cross-document, temporal questions, it collapses.

1.1 The numbers

Two papers anchor the verified evidence. Han et al. (MSU / Meta / IBM, Feb 2025) systematically evaluated GraphRAG variants against vanilla RAG on standard QA benchmarks. The GraphRAG-Bench (arXiv 2506.05690v3) ran in parallel. Across both, the pattern is identical.

The HippoRAG2 paper credits its complex-question lead to its ability to "connect information across distant text segments, crucial for multi-hop reasoning." The MultiHop-RAG temporal result is the most consequential for KYC: a 23-point gap on time-aware questions, then a further 28-point lift from hybrid integration.

1.2 The collapse that explains everything

The Natural Questions row deserves its own treatment. KG-only GraphRAG drops to 32.18% on a benchmark where basic RAG hits 86.7%. Why? Han et al. measured directly: only 65.5–65.8% of answer entities are present in the constructed KG. The KG simply lost a third of the world during ingestion.

Evidence · the binding constraint

If extraction misses an entity, no amount of retriever sophistication recovers it. For a KYC graph, this means: the marginal dollar of engineering effort should flow to extraction quality and entity resolution before retriever cleverness. A perfectly tuned retriever over a 65%-complete graph yields a 65%-correct system. A mediocre retriever over a 95%-complete graph crushes it.

1.3 The five failure modes of vector-only RAG, made concrete

1.3.1 Joinability

Vector search can find "documents that mention John Smith." It cannot answer "is John Smith on the passport the same John Smith on the bank statement?" That is an entity-resolution question with a calibrated posterior, not a cosine-similarity question. A retriever returns chunks; an analyst asking that question needs a decision: same person at 0.97 confidence, with these features matching and these not. Vector stores do not produce that artifact.

1.3.2 Cross-document reasoning

"Does the name on the ID match the name on the registration match the name on the bank statement?" requires three retrievals and a structured comparison. A single vector hit returns one chunk; the agent has to plan a multi-step retrieval and then do the comparison itself, in-context, on raw strings. Every step adds hallucination risk. The graph version is a single Cypher query that returns three normalized name strings and a similarity score.

1.3.3 Temporal correctness

"Who worked at Company X in the last three years?" is a query over edge attributes with interval intersection. It has no good vector representation. You can embed CVs and search for "people who worked at Company X," but the temporal filter — "in the last three years" — has to happen post-retrieval against parsed dates that may not be in the chunk you retrieved. The graph version is one Cypher MATCH.

1.3.4 Provenance

A vector hit gives you a chunk and a similarity score. It does not give you: which extraction run produced this chunk, which model version, against which source byte stream, at what timestamp, with what bounding box on what page, validated by which rule. For regulated work, the chain of custody is the deliverable. Vector stores do not carry it natively.

1.3.5 Query routing

Simple fact lookups dominate volume in any real system. Sending every query through GraphRAG regresses both latency and accuracy on the easy 80%. A production system needs an upstream classifier — "is this a simple lookup or a multi-hop reasoning task?" — and routes accordingly. Vector-only stacks have no native handle for this; the classifier becomes a separate, hand-built component.

1.4 The dominant pattern: hybrid, not graph-only

The MultiHop-RAG hybrid result — 71.17% → 77.62% — is the empirical justification for hybrid. The right architecture is not "replace vectors with graphs." It is:

  • Vector + BM25 for fast lexical and semantic recall over text chunks.
  • Graph traversal for joinable, multi-hop, temporal reasoning.
  • A planner that decomposes the user's question into a sequence of retrievals across both, and a comparator that does the final synthesis.
  • Provenance threaded through every step.

1.5 What this means for your KYC build

Concrete implications, in priority order:

  1. Spend extraction-quality budget before retriever budget. Better OCR, better grammar-constrained decoding, dual-LLM verification. The 65% KG-completeness ceiling is your real bottleneck.
  2. Build a query router from day one. Classify incoming requests into lookup, multi-document validation, temporal reasoning, aggregate. Route each to the appropriate path.
  3. Never present a retrieved chunk without its span citation. Bbox, page, OCR confidence, extractor version. This is what makes the artifact defensible.
  4. Test multi-hop questions explicitly in your eval set. A KYC system that scores 95% on "find the customer's date of birth" but 40% on "is the name on the ID consistent with the registration and the statement" is failing where it matters.

Chapter 2 · Part I

Document understanding beyond OCR

Reading list · vendor matrix ~60 min read

"OCR" is a 1990s framing. What you actually need is a pipeline that recovers the structure of a document — layout regions, reading order, table cells with row/column spans, figure regions, signature crops, stamp detection, MRZ strings on passports, PDF417 barcodes on driver's licenses, and bank-statement transactions normalized into a typed table — and emits all of it with bounding boxes back to the source page, so every downstream extraction carries provenance.

2.1 What "structure" means, document by document

2.1.1 Identity documents

A passport carries three independent data sources that must agree: the Visual Inspection Zone (VIZ — the human-readable fields), the Machine Readable Zone (MRZ — two or three lines of ISO/IEC 7501-1 strings with check digits), and a chip (NFC, optional). A driver's license carries the visual zone and a PDF417 barcode (AAMVA standard) encoding the same fields. A national ID may carry a QR code or a 2D Aztec barcode.

The deterministic path is: decode MRZ and PDF417 with dedicated libraries (pymrz, pylibdmtx, zxing), validate check digits, and then cross-check field-by-field against the VIZ extracted by OCR. Mismatches are red flags — either a forgery, a damaged document, or an OCR error worth a human review.

Pattern · the three-way check

For every ID, compute field_value_MRZ, field_value_VIZ, field_value_chip (if available). Score agreement per field. A passport where MRZ surname is "SMITH" but VIZ is "SHITH" is almost certainly an OCR error; a passport where MRZ check digit fails is almost certainly tampered. Surface both as distinct flags with distinct severities.

2.1.2 Bank statements

The hard problem is not OCR — it is table reconstruction across page boundaries with running-balance reconciliation. A six-page statement is one logical table; OCR returns six images with headers and footers that need to be stripped, columns that may shift width across pages, and transaction rows that occasionally wrap. The output schema you actually need:

class BankStatement(BaseModel):
    account_holder_name: str
    account_holder_address: Address
    account_number: str
    iban: str | None
    bic: str | None
    statement_period_start: date
    statement_period_end: date
    opening_balance: Decimal
    closing_balance: Decimal
    currency: Literal["EUR", "USD", "GBP", ...]
    transactions: list[Transaction]

class Transaction(BaseModel):
    booking_date: date
    value_date: date | None
    description: str
    counterparty_name: str | None
    counterparty_iban: str | None
    debit: Decimal | None
    credit: Decimal | None
    running_balance: Decimal | None
    source_span: Span  # page, bbox, raw_text

Reconciliation: opening_balance + Σ(credit − debit) == closing_balance. If it doesn't, you have a parsing error and you must flag the document for review rather than load it into the graph. This is the single highest-leverage validation in bank-statement extraction.

2.1.3 Business registrations

Per jurisdiction. UK Companies House provides clean structured data via API; many other registries are PDFs of varying quality, in local languages, with country-specific corporate forms (GmbH vs SARL vs S.A. vs LLC vs Ltd). Extract: legal name, registration number, jurisdiction, incorporation date, registered address, registered agent, current officers (directors, secretaries), share capital, status (active / dissolved / struck off), and ultimate beneficial owners (where disclosed).

Cross-reference every business against LEI (GLEIF) and OpenCorporates by jurisdiction + registration number. Where the registry exposes UBO data (EU 5AMLD jurisdictions), pull it; where it doesn't (US, Delaware), you'll need a third-party (Sayari, Moody's Orbis, or direct disclosure from the customer).

2.3 The recommended ingestion topology

┌────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐ │ Source documents (PDF, JPEG, PNG, HEIC, TIFF, DOCX) │ │ ↓ │ │ Content-addressable storage (R2/S3), keyed by sha256(bytes) │ │ ↓ │ │ Doc classifier (passport vs DL vs statement vs registration) │ │ ↓ │ │ Parallel parsers: │ │ ├─ Cloud OCR (Textract or Azure DI) → layout + tables │ │ ├─ Docling / Reducto (fallback) → table cross-check │ │ └─ Deterministic (MRZ, PDF417, QR) → for IDs only │ │ ↓ │ │ Parser output: │ │ { pages: [{ regions, tables, signatures, stamps, │ │ text, bboxes, ocr_confidence }], │ │ barcodes: [...], │ │ parser_version: "...", source_hash: "..." } │ │ ↓ │ │ Persist as immutable artifact, keyed by │ │ (source_hash, parser_version) │ └────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘

Three properties of this topology to internalize:

  • Parallel parsers, not fallback chains. Run two parsers on financial pages and compare. Disagreement is a signal — investigate, do not silently pick one.
  • Deterministic where you can. MRZ and barcodes are deterministic; never run them through an LLM. The LLM is for what cannot be parsed deterministically.
  • Immutable artifacts. Never overwrite a parser output. Every parse becomes a new artifact keyed by (source_hash, parser_version, parsed_at). This is what lets you replay the past when parsers improve.

2.4 What an "AI-native" extraction differs in

Incumbent OCR vendors emit unstructured-ish text plus tables. An AI-native pipeline emits typed, validated, span-cited domain objects directly:

  • Not "text with bounding boxes" but PassportExtraction objects with check-digit-validated MRZ.
  • Not "table cells" but Transaction[] with reconciled running balances.
  • Not "form fields" but BusinessRegistration objects with LEI cross-reference attempted.
  • Every field carrying {page, bbox, ocr_confidence, extractor_model_version}.

This is the seam between Chapters 2 and 3: document understanding ends with structured artifacts; deterministic extraction begins with constrained-decoded LLMs turning OCR output into those artifacts under a schema you can defend.

Chapter 3 · Part I

Deterministic information extraction

Evidenced (decoding layer) ~75 min read

This is the chapter where the founder's defensibility lives. Document understanding (Chapter 2) tells you where on the page the data is. Deterministic extraction tells you what it means, with what confidence, and produces an artifact you can hand to a regulator.

3.1 The problem with "ask the LLM nicely"

The naive pattern — feed the OCR output to GPT-4 with a prompt like "extract the fields as JSON" — fails in production for predictable reasons: malformed JSON, hallucinated fields, missing required fields, type coercion errors (date strings in 12 different formats), missing source citations, and silent degradation when the document drifts. The cost is not just wasted tokens. It is bad data flowing into the graph and corrupting downstream ER and validation.

3.2 The three layers of determinism

Layer 1 · Constrained decoding

Force the LLM to emit text that cannot violate the schema. Implemented at the decode loop: at every token step, mask the logits to only allow tokens that keep the partial output within the JSON-schema (or context-free grammar) language. Output that is invalid JSON literally cannot be sampled.

Evidence · constrained decoding is no longer a tax

XGrammar (CMU + MLC, arXiv 2411.15100, MLC blog Nov 2024) reports up to 3.5× faster JSON-schema logits masking and up to 10× faster CFG-guided masking vs Outlines / llama.cpp / lm-format-enforcer / vLLM. End-to-end on Llama-3-8B / H100: up to 14× for JSON-schema, up to 80× for CFG-guided. Per-token latency < 40 µs (independent benchmark, arXiv 2501.10868).

Read as: "constrained decoding is no longer a latency tax." Do not read as "guaranteed 14× speedup" — these are author-run "up to" numbers against baselines that have since improved. The architectural conclusion holds regardless of where the leaderboard stands today.

Two layers to distinguish: grammar engines (mask the logits at decode time) and typed-output libraries (wrap vendor APIs with Pydantic / Zod validation, retry-on-violation loops, and ergonomic syntax). Most production teams use both — a typed-output library at the application layer, calling an engine that does the actual constrained decoding under the hood.

Grammar engines

  • XGrammar — best published numbers, JSON-schema and CFG, broad engine support.
  • Outlines — Python-first, integrates with vLLM and Transformers; mature ecosystem.
  • llguidance — Microsoft Guidance's grammar engine; very flexible.
  • lm-format-enforcer — JSON-schema focused, simple.
  • Vendor APIs — OpenAI's response_format=json_schema with strict:true, Anthropic's tool-use with strict schemas, Google's controlled generation. These work; the cost vs accuracy tradeoff vs self-hosting is a separate question.

Layer 2 · Pydantic / Zod validation

Constrained decoding gets you syntactically valid JSON. Semantic validation is a second layer: regex on identifier formats, range checks on dates, enum membership on document types, cross-field invariants. Pydantic v2 in Python and Zod in TypeScript are the canonical tools.

from pydantic import BaseModel, Field, field_validator
from typing import Literal
from datetime import date

class Span(BaseModel):
    page: int = Field(ge=1)
    bbox: tuple[float, float, float, float]  # x0,y0,x1,y1
    ocr_text: str
    ocr_confidence: float = Field(ge=0, le=1)
    extractor_model: str
    extractor_version: str

class FieldValue[T](BaseModel):
    value: T
    span: Span
    extraction_confidence: float = Field(ge=0, le=1)

class PassportExtraction(BaseModel):
    document_type: Literal["passport"]
    issuing_country: FieldValue[str]  # ISO 3166-1 alpha-3
    surname: FieldValue[str]
    given_names: FieldValue[str]
    document_number: FieldValue[str]
    nationality: FieldValue[str]
    date_of_birth: FieldValue[date]
    sex: FieldValue[Literal["M", "F", "X"]]
    date_of_issue: FieldValue[date]
    date_of_expiry: FieldValue[date]
    mrz_line_1: FieldValue[str]
    mrz_line_2: FieldValue[str]
    mrz_check_digit_status: Literal["all_valid", "some_invalid", "all_invalid"]

    @field_validator("date_of_expiry")
    def expiry_after_issue(cls, v, info):
        issue = info.data.get("date_of_issue")
        if issue and v.value <= issue.value:
            raise ValueError("expiry must be after issue")
        return v

The FieldValue[T] wrapper is the unsung hero. Every leaf field carries its span citation by construction. There is no path in the codebase where a value exists without provenance, because the type system forbids it.

Layer 3 · Dual-LLM verification

Run extraction with model A (e.g., Claude Sonnet). Then run a verifier prompt with model B (e.g., GPT-4o or Gemini), giving it both the source document and A's extraction, asking: "list every field where the extraction does not match the document, with the correct value and the exact text from the document that supports it." Disagreements escalate to human review.

The economic argument: KG-incompleteness is the binding constraint (Chapter 1). Each missed or mis-extracted entity is a future graph join failure. Paying for a second LLM pass on every document is cheap relative to the cost of an incorrect KYC decision.

Pattern · the agreement matrix

Log every (model_A_value, model_B_value, ocr_text) tuple per field. Over time, this gives you a per-field reliability matrix: which fields routinely disagree (signature dates, multi-name formats, transliterated names) and which never do. Use the matrix to choose where dual-verification is cost-effective and where it is theatre.

3.3 Span-grounded extraction in practice

The hard part of span-grounded extraction is making the LLM emit accurate bounding boxes. Options, in order of robustness:

  1. OCR-first: the OCR layer produces tokens with bboxes. The extractor selects which OCR tokens become which extracted field. Bbox is the union of selected token bboxes. This is the most reliable.
  2. Coordinate-aware LLM: feed the LLM a textual representation of the page with explicit [bbox] annotations per text run; the LLM emits a field with the [bbox] tag it chose. Works but requires careful prompting.
  3. Vision-language model: feed the page image plus a request for bbox coordinates. State of the art (GPT-4o, Gemini, Claude with vision) is now usable for this. Validate the bbox by re-OCRing the region and checking string equality.

3.4 Failure modes you will hit

  • Schema rigidity. A required field that is missing in 5% of documents forces the extractor into hallucination. Mark fields Optional liberally; let the validation layer flag missingness.
  • Date ambiguity. "03/04/2025" is March 4 in the US and April 3 in the EU. Always extract dates as {raw_text, parsed_iso, parser_locale} and apply locale at validation, not extraction.
  • Multi-name structures. Spanish double surnames (Garcia Lopez), Arabic kunya / nasab / nisba, Hungarian surname-first, transliterated forms. Extract {name_as_appears, surname_components[], given_components[]} and let ER normalize.
  • Currency drift. A statement that mixes EUR and USD line items. Extract currency per transaction, not per statement.
  • OCR noise propagating to extraction. If ocr_confidence < 0.85 on a critical field, the extraction is suspect even if the LLM is confident. Compose both confidences.

3.5 What you ship

An extraction service that:

  • Accepts a content-addressed source-document hash and a target schema name.
  • Routes to the right parser, runs constrained-decoded extraction, runs dual-LLM verification, runs Pydantic validation, runs cross-field rules.
  • Emits an immutable, signed extraction artifact keyed by (source_hash, schema_name, extractor_version, verifier_version).
  • Returns the artifact and a structured event for downstream pipelines.
  • Never silently fails — every error is a typed event with the field, the failure mode, and the recommended human action.

This artifact is what flows into entity resolution (Chapter 4) and onward to the graph. It is also what you produce on demand for the regulator.

Chapter 4 · Part I

Entity resolution at scale

Evidenced ~90 min read

You have structured extractions from a passport, a bank statement, a business registration, and an application form. Each names a "John Smith." Are they the same person? That question — answered with calibrated probability, audit trail, and tunable thresholds — is entity resolution, and it is the second pillar of the spine.

4.1 Fellegi–Sunter, derived from scratch

Fellegi and Sunter published their record-linkage framework in 1969. Six decades later, it is still the canonical foundation, because the math is simple, the assumptions are explicit, and the output is a calibrated probability rather than a black-box score.

The setup. You have two records, each with a vector of fields (name, date of birth, address, etc.). For each field, you define a comparison — exact match, fuzzy match, mismatch — and observe an outcome γ. The question: given the vector of comparison outcomes γ = (γ₁, γ₂, …, γ_k), what is the probability the two records refer to the same entity?

Define:

  • m_i = P(γ_i | match) — probability of observing comparison outcome i given the records refer to the same entity.
  • u_i = P(γ_i | non-match) — probability of observing comparison outcome i given the records refer to different entities.
  • λ = P(match) — overall prior probability that any candidate pair is a true match.

By Bayes:

P(match | γ)     P(γ | match) · P(match)     λ      ∏ m_i
─────────────  = ───────────────────────── = ───── · ───────
P(non-match | γ) P(γ | non-match) · P(non-match) (1-λ)  ∏ u_i

Take log₂:

log₂ Odds(match | γ) = log₂(λ/(1-λ)) + Σ log₂(m_i / u_i)

That is the whole framework. The per-field match weight is log₂(m_i / u_i), the weights are additive across fields under conditional independence, and the prior λ sets the base rate. Convert log-odds to posterior probability with the logistic function.

Evidence · this is the production foundation

Verified against Splink documentation (moj-analytical-services.github.io/splink): "weight = log₂(m/u) Bayes factor, match weights are additive, three core parameters λ, m, u." This matches Fellegi & Sunter (1969) line for line. The framework is not vendor-specific; it is the math.

4.2 Why the math matters in production

Because the weights are additive, you can read a match decision. "The 0.97 posterior came from: +12.3 bits for exact DOB match, +8.1 bits for fuzzy name match, +3.2 bits for partial address match, −2.4 bits for nationality mismatch." A regulator looking at a flagged transaction can see exactly which features fired. No other ER framework gives you this transparency.

4.3 Splink: the production OSS implementation

Splink (UK Ministry of Justice) implements Fellegi–Sunter on:

  • Backends: DuckDB, Spark, Athena, SQLite, Postgres.
  • Scale: 100M+ records (production deployments at the UK Office for National Statistics, NHS).
  • Blocking rules: reduce candidate-pair generation from O(n²) to O(n·k). Examples: "block on first 3 chars of surname + year of birth," "block on postcode prefix." Multiple blocking rules union together — a pair only needs to satisfy one.
  • EM training: Expectation-Maximization estimates m and u from unlabeled data, given good blocking rules and a starting prior.
  • Visualization: interactive cluster inspection, weight bar charts, match-weight waterfalls.

A minimal Splink configuration for KYC:

from splink import Linker, SettingsCreator, block_on
import splink.comparison_library as cl

settings = SettingsCreator(
    link_type="dedupe_only",
    blocking_rules_to_generate_predictions=[
        block_on("substr(surname,1,3)", "year_of_birth"),
        block_on("postcode_prefix", "substr(given_names,1,1)"),
        block_on("document_number"),
    ],
    comparisons=[
        cl.JaroWinklerAtThresholds("surname", [0.92, 0.88]),
        cl.JaroWinklerAtThresholds("given_names", [0.92, 0.85]),
        cl.DateOfBirthComparison("date_of_birth"),
        cl.LevenshteinAtThresholds("address_line", [2, 5]),
        cl.ExactMatch("nationality").configure(term_frequency_adjustments=True),
    ],
    retain_intermediate_calculation_columns=True,
)

linker = Linker(records_df, settings, db_api=duckdb_api)
linker.training.estimate_u_using_random_sampling(max_pairs=1e7)
linker.training.estimate_parameters_using_expectation_maximisation(
    block_on("date_of_birth")
)
predictions = linker.inference.predict(threshold_match_probability=0.9)

Notes:

  • Comparison levels. Each field gets multiple comparison levels (exact, near-exact, fuzzy, distant). Each level has its own m and u. The framework naturally handles "fuzzy matches are weaker evidence than exact matches."
  • Term frequency adjustments. "John" agreeing on first name is weaker evidence than "Xinran" agreeing, because the former is more common. Splink can adjust per-value based on observed frequency in your data.
  • EM convergence. Watch for trivial solutions. EM needs at least one blocking rule it can use to estimate m from match-rich subsets.

4.5 The hard parts of names

4.5.1 Transliteration

"Mohamed" matches "Mohammed" matches "Muhammad" matches "Mohamad" matches "Mohammad." Six common Latin renderings of محمد. Treating these as a mismatch destroys recall in any system handling Arabic-speaking populations. Three approaches:

  • Normalize at ingest. Use ICU transliteration + a custom transliteration table to canonical forms. Brittle when you encounter new forms.
  • Penalty-soft in u. Train m and u on data where transliterated variants count as matches; the framework absorbs the variation into u automatically.
  • Phonetic encoding. Soundex / Metaphone / Double-Metaphone / Beider-Morse. Compare phonetic encodings as a separate comparison level.

In practice, combine all three: normalize at ingest, expose phonetic-match as a comparison level, and let EM tune the weights.

4.5.2 Cultural name structure

  • Spanish double surnames. "García López" — both are surnames; "García" is paternal, "López" is maternal. Documents may include only one.
  • Arabic kunya/ism/nasab/nisba. "Abu Yusuf Muhammad ibn Ahmad al-Baghdadi" decomposes into a teknonym, given name, patronym, and place-of-origin. A passport may show any subset.
  • Hungarian and East Asian. Surname first.
  • Patronymic systems. Russian "Ivanovich" / "Ivanovna" derive from the father's first name.
  • Married-name updates. A statement under the maiden name and an ID under the married name. ER must handle both with documented support.

The pragmatic move: extract {name_as_appears, surname_components[], given_components[], cultural_hint} at extraction (Chapter 3), and let the ER compare on any pair of components.

4.5.3 Address normalization

libpostal (OSS, parses ~60 languages) breaks an address string into {house_number, road, unit, postcode, city, state, country}. Compare components, not strings. "12 Rue Émile Zola" vs "12, rue Emile Zola, 75015 Paris" becomes a structured match.

4.6 Corporate entity matching

Different problem from person matching, because companies have authoritative IDs.

  • LEI (Legal Entity Identifier, GLEIF): 20-character ISO 17442; the only universal ID for corporates. Free API. Coverage is strongest for financial-market participants; weaker for small businesses.
  • OpenCorporates: largest open registry crosswalk; jurisdiction + registration number → canonical record. Free API at low volume; paid for production.
  • Wikidata Q-IDs: last resort for well-known entities; not exhaustive.

The resolution policy: for every extracted business name, attempt LEI lookup first, OpenCorporates by jurisdiction + reg-number second, fuzzy match against your own canonical-company table third. Persist the resolution chain — which lookups succeeded, which failed, with what confidence.

Recommendation

4.9 ER as a service in the platform

Architecturally, ER is a service that:

  • Accepts a stream of extracted records.
  • For each incoming record, blocks against the existing canonical table.
  • Scores candidate pairs with Splink.
  • Routes by threshold: auto-merge, auto-link-flag, human-review, distinct.
  • Emits ER decisions as immutable events: {record_id_a, record_id_b, posterior, weights_breakdown, decision, resolver_version, resolved_at}.
  • Re-runs in the background as new evidence arrives (a new document may shift a posterior from 0.85 to 0.96).

The "weights breakdown" is what makes regulator-facing analyst UIs possible (Chapter 17). Click a "same person" link in the graph, see the waterfall of per-feature weights that produced the posterior.

5.3a KG-as-a-service — the external option

You can also buy a knowledge graph. Diffbot has been building a continuously-updated web-scale KG (people, organizations, products, articles) via crawl + extraction for over a decade, accessible via API. Google Knowledge Graph API covers the well-known entities. For your KYC and CV use cases, these are complements, not replacements — useful for entity enrichment (canonicalize a company name against Diffbot's organization graph) but not for storing your own customer facts.

Pattern: at extraction time, for every business or person name, query Diffbot or Google KG → if a high-confidence match exists, attach the external entity ID to your canonical node. Your graph becomes federated with the public web without leaving your control plane.

5.4 Named graphs — the provenance trick that pays for itself

Every fact in your graph should belong to a named graph keyed by (extraction_run_id, source_document_hash, extractor_model_version). This single discipline gives you:

  • Replay. Re-run extraction with a new model? Load the new facts into a new named graph; queries can compare old vs new.
  • Retraction. A document is later discovered to be fraudulent? Drop its named graph; the facts cease to exist for queries that filter by valid graphs.
  • ACL. User A can only see documents tagged with their org? Filter retrieval by allowed named graphs.
  • "As-of" knowledge. Want to know what the system believed at a point in time? Filter to named graphs ingested before that time.

In Neo4j, named graphs are typically modeled as a label or a property on the relationship; in RDF they are first-class quads. The mechanism is less important than the discipline of never letting a fact exist without a source.

5.5 Coreference and event extraction

Two extraction sub-problems that the literature underrates.

5.5.1 Coreference

Within a single CV: "Born in 1985, John Smith joined Acme in 2010. He led the engineering team. Smith then moved to Globex in 2014." The pronouns "He" and the bare surname "Smith" all refer to the same Person. Modern coref models (SpanBERT, LingMess) handle this well at the sentence level; LLMs handle it cross-paragraph if you prompt for it. Output: {mention_id → canonical_within_doc_id}.

Cross-document coreference is entity resolution (Chapter 4). Within-document coreference is a Chapter-3 extraction concern.

5.5.2 Event extraction

"Joined Acme in January 2010 as a Junior Engineer, promoted to Senior in 2012, left in March 2014." Three events with start/end intervals and role labels. Model as edges with attributes, not nodes:

(:Person {id: 42})-[:WORKED_AT {
  role: "Junior Engineer",
  start_valid: date("2010-01"),
  end_valid: date("2012-12"),
  source_doc: "cv:hash:abc..."
}]->(:Organization {id: "acme"})

(:Person {id: 42})-[:WORKED_AT {
  role: "Senior Engineer",
  start_valid: date("2013-01"),
  end_valid: date("2014-03"),
  source_doc: "cv:hash:abc..."
}]->(:Organization {id: "acme"})

One person can have many WORKED_AT edges to the same organization — different roles, different intervals. This is what enables the "who worked at Company X in the last 3 years" query in Chapter 13.

5.6 The minimum viable KYC graph schema

// Core nodes
(:Person {id, canonical_name, dob, nationality, created_at})
(:Business {id, canonical_name, lei, registration_country, status})
(:Document {id, doc_type, source_hash, ingested_at, parser_version})
(:Address {id, normalized, country})
(:ScreeningHit {id, list, list_version, match_score, retrieved_at, valid_at})
(:Case {id, status, opened_at, decided_at, decided_by})
(:ExtractionRun {id, extractor_version, started_at, finished_at})

// Customer ↔ document association
(:Customer)-[:HAS_DOCUMENT]->(:Document)

// Document asserts a fact about an entity, with span citation
(:Document)-[:ASSERTS {
  field, page, bbox, ocr_confidence, extractor_confidence,
  extraction_run_id
}]->(:Person|:Business|:Address)

// ER decision linking two extracted entities to the same canonical
(:Person)-[:RESOLVED_TO {
  weight, posterior, resolver_version, resolved_at,
  feature_breakdown
}]->(:Person)

// Screening hit with bitemporal validity
(:Person)-[:MATCHED_BY_SCREENING {
  match_score, valid_at, invalid_at, list_version
}]->(:ScreeningHit)

// Case structure
(:Case)-[:ABOUT]->(:Customer)
(:Case)-[:HAS_EVIDENCE]->(:Document)
(:Case)-[:DECIDED_BY]->(:User)

Five properties of this schema worth internalizing:

  1. Every ASSERTS edge carries the span citation. The graph never loses where a fact came from.
  2. RESOLVED_TO is a relationship with weights. ER decisions are reversible if a future extraction shifts the posterior.
  3. Screening hits have valid_at. Lists update; a hit is true only at a time. Bitemporal (Chapter 16).
  4. Cases bundle evidence and a decision. Auditable atomic unit.
  5. No facts exist outside an ExtractionRun. Replayability and provenance baked in.

6.2 Heuristics for your specific use cases

Live KYC memory (10–100M edges, bitemporal, < 500ms reads)

Pick: Neo4j (community or AuraDB), KuzuDB embedded inside your extraction service, or FalkorDB. All three handle the read latency. Neo4j wins on ecosystem; KuzuDB wins on ops simplicity (it's a library); FalkorDB wins on raw speed.

CV / talent graph (10–100M nodes, frequent co-worker overlap queries)

Pick: Neo4j with well-designed indices on company canonical IDs, or KuzuDB for analytical workloads. The query pattern is "match on a canonical company, traverse to people, intersect intervals." Indices on (company.canonical_id) and (WORKED_AT.start_valid, WORKED_AT.end_valid) are what make this fast.

Policy / corporate documents (need access control over the graph)

Pick: Stardog or GraphDB if you need OWL reasoning over access policies. Otherwise Neo4j with OPA in the retrieval path (Chapter 14).

Curriculum (per-year isolation, prerequisite graphs)

Pick: TerminusDB if branching-per-cohort fits your mental model, otherwise property graph with named-graph-per-cohort.

6.3 Embedded vs server — the under-appreciated axis

KuzuDB and (to some extent) FalkorDB-via-Redis-embedded are embedded graph databases. They live in your service's process. This matters because:

  • No network hop on every query → P99 latencies drop by 10–50×.
  • No cluster to operate, no failover, no version upgrades coordinated with your service.
  • The trade-off: a single process limit; no concurrent multi-service access; backups are file copies.

For an extraction service that loads facts as part of processing a document, an embedded graph DB inside the service is dramatically simpler. The "central graph" then becomes an analytical replica (Neo4j cluster, or a lakehouse table) that the embedded process periodically syncs to.

6.4 OLTP vs OLAP graph workloads

Two different shapes:

  • OLTP graph — "give me this case's facts," "screen this person," "merge these two entities." Point reads, small traversals (≤ 3 hops), milliseconds. Neo4j, FalkorDB, Memgraph, KuzuDB.
  • OLAP graph — "which company in our portfolio has the densest cluster of shell-company links," "find all PageRank-top-10 sanctioned beneficial owners." Deep traversals, full-graph algorithms (PageRank, community detection, centrality), seconds to minutes. TigerGraph, AnzoGraph, KuzuDB analytical, RelationalAI, or Neo4j GDS library.

For a KYC platform you need both. Run OLTP on the live store; replicate to an OLAP store for periodic analytics (network risk scoring, UBO chain discovery).

6.5 The zero-ETL pattern — Puppygraph and friends

Puppygraph and similar tools (RelationalAI, even Trino's graph plugins) implement a different paradigm: do not move the data; query relational tables or Iceberg files as a graph. The graph layer is a virtual mapping from tables to nodes/edges, executed at query time.

When this pays off:

  • Your facts already live in a well-modeled lakehouse and you don't want to maintain a second store.
  • Most queries are simple (1–2 hops).
  • You want graph queries without an ETL pipeline.

When it doesn't:

  • You need deep traversals (5+ hops) — the underlying SQL becomes brutal.
  • You need OLTP-class latencies — query rewriting adds a tax.
  • You need graph algorithms (PageRank, community detection) — these need a real graph engine.

7.2 The retrieval modes — and when each fires

7.2.1 Local retrieval

Start from an entity (or a small set of entities), traverse 1–3 hops, collect attached facts. "Tell me about Person X" → start at X, traverse to documents that assert about X, traverse to screening hits, traverse to related people. Returns a subgraph as context.

7.2.2 Global retrieval (community-summary)

Microsoft GraphRAG's signature mode. Pre-compute community detection (Leiden, Louvain) over the graph; LLM-summarize each community offline; at query time, retrieve top-k community summaries by relevance, map-reduce them into a final answer. Excellent for "what are the major themes" questions; expensive; not useful for "is this name a match" questions.

7.2.3 Hybrid retrieval

Vector store on chunk embeddings + BM25 on chunk text + graph traversal from entities mentioned in chunks. Fuse the three with reciprocal rank fusion (RRF) or learned reranking. This is the empirically dominant pattern.

7.2.4 Path-based retrieval (PathRAG)

Find paths in the graph that connect query entities. "How is Person A connected to Sanctioned Entity Z?" → enumerate paths; rank by length / weight; return paths as context.

7.2.5 Agentic / planner-driven retrieval

An LLM planner decomposes the question into sub-queries, routes each to the appropriate retriever (vector, BM25, graph, SQL, external API), composes results. This is what "agentic RAG" properly means.

7.4 Query decomposition and multi-hop planning

A real KYC question: "Is the company on this business registration ultimately owned by anyone on the OFAC list?"

The planner must decompose:

  1. Extract the business name from the registration → resolve to a canonical Organization node.
  2. Traverse OWNED_BY edges from the canonical Organization (1..N hops, transitively).
  3. For each owner reached, check MATCHED_BY_SCREENING against current OFAC list.
  4. Return the path if any hit found.

This is not a vector search problem. The closest you can get with pure RAG is "retrieve documents about this company and its owners and hope an LLM stitches it together," which is exactly the failure pattern Chapter 1 documents. The graph version is one Cypher query.

MATCH path = (b:Business {id: $bizId})-[:OWNED_BY*1..6]->(o:Person|:Business)
MATCH (o)-[:MATCHED_BY_SCREENING]->(h:ScreeningHit)
WHERE h.list = "OFAC" AND h.valid_at <= datetime() AND
      (h.invalid_at IS NULL OR h.invalid_at > datetime())
RETURN path, h
LIMIT 10

7.5 Community-summary retrieval, made concrete

For a portfolio of 100,000 KYC cases, Microsoft GraphRAG can answer "what are the top risk themes this quarter?" by:

  1. Detecting communities in the case-graph (Leiden algorithm).
  2. Summarizing each community with an LLM (offline, batch).
  3. At query time, retrieving top-k community summaries by query relevance.
  4. Map-reducing them into a final report.

The cost is real — recomputing communities and summaries when the graph changes is expensive. Use for quarterly compliance reports, not for "is this customer suspect."

7.6 The recommendation, in one rule

Architecture

For live KYC and CV use cases: Graphiti-style incremental bitemporal KG with hybrid retrieval (vector + BM25 + graph), no LLM at retrieval, planner-decomposed multi-hop queries. Reserve Microsoft GraphRAG-style community summaries for periodic global reports. The two are complementary, not competitive.

8.3 Where vector, lexical, and graph fuse

User query ↓ Query planner (LLM) │ ├─→ Vector store ┐ │ (semantic chunks)│ │ │ ├─→ BM25 lexical │ Top-k candidates per modality │ (exact terms) │ │ │ ├─→ Graph traversal │ │ (entity/relation)│ │ │ └─→ SQL / external API┘ ↓ Reciprocal Rank Fusion ↓ Re-ranker (ColBERT / cross-encoder) ↓ Top-N context to LLM

Three practical points:

  • Reciprocal Rank Fusion beats hand-tuned weights in most cases. score(d) = Σ 1 / (k + rank_i(d)) with k ≈ 60. Simple and robust.
  • Re-ranking pays. A cross-encoder (ColBERT, BGE-reranker, Cohere Rerank) over the top-100 candidates lifts recall@10 by 5–15 points consistently across benchmarks.
  • Chunk metadata is the glue. Every chunk carries {document_id, named_graph_id, entity_ids_mentioned, customer_id, ACL}. The graph layer reads chunk → entity references to traverse; the ACL layer filters at retrieval time.

8.4 Embedding model selection — a brief practical note

The embedding-model landscape changes fast. Heuristics that age well:

  • For English, bge-large-en-v1.5, jina-embeddings-v3, OpenAI text-embedding-3-large, Cohere embed-v3 are all production-grade.
  • For multilingual KYC populations, bge-m3 and Cohere embed-multilingual-v3 dominate; mE5-large also works.
  • For domain-specific (legal, medical, financial), fine-tune a base model on contrastive pairs from your corpus. The gain is real but expensive to maintain.
  • Dimensions: 768 or 1024 is the sweet spot; bigger costs storage and indexing without proportional accuracy gain. Matryoshka embeddings (with progressive truncation) let you index high-dim and serve low-dim.

10.3 The rule + LLM-judge hybrid

Rules cover the deterministic 90% of cases. LLM judges handle the ambiguous 10%.

Deterministic rules (OPA / Rego)

  • date_of_birth(ID) == date_of_birth(application) — exact match required.
  • mrz_check_digits_valid(passport) — boolean check.
  • opening + Σ(credits − debits) == closing on every bank statement page chain.
  • nationality(ID) == nationality(application).
  • document_expiry > today for every required document.

LLM judges (constrained-decoded, structured output)

  • Address normalization tie-breakers: "12 Rue Émile Zola" vs "12 R. Emile Zola Apt 3" — judge: "do these refer to the same building? Y/N + reason + confidence."
  • Name structure judgments: Spanish double surnames where one document includes only the paternal — judge: "is this consistent with the same person? Y/N + reason."
  • Signature similarity reasoning: visual model returns 0.78 similarity; judge: "given the document type, time gap, and visual score, is this acceptable? Y/N + reason."
  • Source-of-funds narratives: "is the customer's stated occupation consistent with the transaction pattern on the statement?"

Every LLM judge call returns a structured verdict with reason and confidence — schema-enforced (Chapter 3). The verdict, the reasoning, and the model version all join the case's evidence chain.

10.4 The multi-document consistency query — concrete

The single most important validation in any KYC system: "do the names match across the three core documents?"

MATCH (c:Customer {id: $custId})
MATCH (c)-[:HAS_DOCUMENT]->(id_doc:Document {doc_type: "passport"})
MATCH (c)-[:HAS_DOCUMENT]->(bs:Document {doc_type: "bank_statement"})
MATCH (c)-[:HAS_DOCUMENT]->(reg:Document {doc_type: "business_registration"})

MATCH (id_doc)-[a1:ASSERTS {field: "name"}]->(p1:Person)
MATCH (bs)-[a2:ASSERTS {field: "account_holder"}]->(p2:Person)
MATCH (reg)-[a3:ASSERTS {field: "officer"}]->(p3:Person)

// All three must resolve to same canonical person
MATCH (p1)-[:RESOLVED_TO*0..1]->(canon:Person)
MATCH (p2)-[:RESOLVED_TO*0..1]->(canon)
MATCH (p3)-[:RESOLVED_TO*0..1]->(canon)

RETURN canon, p1, p2, p3,
       a1.span AS id_span, a2.span AS bs_span, a3.span AS reg_span

If the query returns a row, the names are consistent through ER. If not, the rule engine flags NAME_CONSISTENCY_FAILED and the analyst sees the three documents side by side with the disagreeing spans highlighted.

10.5 Reconciliation patterns

Bank-statement reconciliation

The customer claims an SME doing €500k annual revenue. The bank statements show €50k of credits over the year. Reconciliation rule: |claimed_revenue − Σ(credits over period)| / claimed_revenue > 0.5 → flag.

This is one of the most powerful signals in KYC. It cannot be faked at the document level (the statement is real or it is forged), and the math is simple. Build this rule first.

Source-of-funds reconciliation

Customer states income is salary from Acme. Bank statement shows monthly recurring credit from "ACME PAYROLL." Reconciliation: pattern-match descriptions, check timing regularity, compare amount to declared salary. Rule fires when patterns disagree.

Address reconciliation

Address on application, address on ID, address on utility bill, address on bank statement. Normalize all four through libpostal, compute pairwise similarity, require ≥ 3 of 4 to be ≥ 0.85 component overlap. If not, escalate.

10.6 Dual-control workflows

Above a risk threshold (configurable per jurisdiction and product), any decision requires two independent reviewers. Both decisions logged; disagreement triggers a tiebreaker by a senior reviewer.

The graph model:

(:Case)-[:REVIEWED_BY {
  decision: "approve" | "reject" | "escalate",
  reviewer_role: "L1" | "L2" | "senior",
  decision_at: datetime,
  rationale: str,
  rules_overridden: list[str]
}]->(:User)

Every decision is a node-with-attributes edge, immutable, with the reviewer's rationale (often free text but always required). When a regulator asks "why did you approve this case?" the answer is a graph traversal that returns the full review trail.

10.7 Audit-trail design — what an inspector wants to see

Imagine the inspector pulls one case. What artifacts must they get?

  1. The case dossier — every document, with hash, source, ingest time.
  2. Every extraction artifact — what the system extracted, by which model version, against which prompt/schema/grammar hash.
  3. Every ER decision — match weights, posterior, model version, threshold in effect at the time.
  4. Every screening result — which list version, when retrieved, what matched.
  5. Every rule that fired — rule ID, rule version, inputs, outputs, severity.
  6. Every LLM judge call — model version, prompt hash, inputs, structured verdict, reasoning.
  7. Every human review — reviewer, decision, rationale, timestamp, prior decisions if overridden.
  8. The final decision — outcome, rule trace, who decided, when.

All of this is reachable from the case node in the graph. Nothing is lost. Nothing requires reconstruction. The audit trail is the operational graph itself.

The product UX hook

The same artifact the regulator wants is the analyst's working surface. Every analyst screen should let them click any number — a posterior, a similarity score, a rule firing — and walk back to the underlying evidence. This is the single most important UX investment for the platform. It is also impossible without the spine you are building.

11.3 How to wire screening into the graph

// Every Person and Organization carries continuous screening
MATCH (p:Person {id: $personId})
OPTIONAL MATCH (p)-[match:MATCHED_BY_SCREENING]->(hit:ScreeningHit)
WHERE hit.list IN ["OFAC", "UN", "EU", "PEP_TIER_1"]
  AND match.confidence > 0.85
  AND hit.valid_at <= datetime()
  AND (hit.invalid_at IS NULL OR hit.invalid_at > datetime())
RETURN p, hit, match.confidence, match.feature_breakdown
ORDER BY match.confidence DESC

Each ScreeningHit is a node — not a transient API response. It carries (source_list, list_version, match_score, match_features, retrieved_at, valid_at, invalid_at). When the upstream list updates, you re-screen and either keep, retire, or create new hits, with the bitemporal valid/invalid intervals telling the full story.

11.4 The Adverse-media problem

Adverse-media is harder than sanctions because the input is unstructured news in many languages and the matching requires NER + topic classification. ComplyAdvantage, Dow Jones, and World-Check do this for you. If you choose to build it (rarely worth it), you need:

  • A news ingestion pipeline (LexisNexis, Factiva, or RSS aggregators).
  • Per-language NER + entity resolution against your customer graph.
  • Topic classification (financial crime, fraud, bribery, terrorism, organized crime, sanctions evasion).
  • Sentiment / severity scoring.
  • Cross-language deduplication.

Five-engineer-year project. Buy it.

11.5 KYB — the business equivalent

Where KYC verifies a person, KYB verifies a business. Differences:

  • Authoritative registries are jurisdiction-specific. UK Companies House, Delaware Division of Corporations, France INPI, Germany Handelsregister. Each has its own data model and freshness.
  • UBO data quality varies wildly. EU 5AMLD jurisdictions disclose UBO publicly; the US generally does not (except via FinCEN's Corporate Transparency Act submissions, which are non-public).
  • Shell companies and layering. A Cayman parent → a Delaware LLC → a Cyprus subsidiary → a UK operating company. Following ownership chains across jurisdictions is the value-add of Sayari, Orbis, and Quantexa.

Wire KYB into the graph as a parallel-structure ER problem: every business resolved to a canonical Organization node, every Organization linked to its registry record, every officer linked back to the corresponding Person node (which is in turn screened).

11.6 Open Banking — the move that changes the equation

This subsection is its own animal. For KYC, the highest-leverage architectural decision you can make in 2026 is recognizing that bank-statement OCR is a workaround. Where Open Banking is available (most of EU/UK via PSD2/PSD3, growing in US via Section 1033 and Plaid Auth/Income, growing in Brazil/Australia/Singapore via local regs), you do not parse the PDF statement at all — you pull the customer's transactions directly from their bank, in structured form, with cryptographic auth that the data is genuine.

Strategic implication

For the bank-statement validation step (Chapter 10 reconciliation, source-of-funds checks, balance reconciliation), an Open Banking pull is strictly superior to OCR-based extraction wherever it is available: regulator-grade provenance built-in, no parser drift, no forgery risk, and the data arrives already structured into the schema you'd want anyway. OCR-based statement parsing should be your fallback, not your primary path.

1. Customer initiates KYC flow with country / bank known.
2. If country supports Open Banking AND bank is on the aggregator's list:
   → Redirect customer to bank-auth flow (Plaid Link / TrueLayer / etc.)
   → Receive structured transactions + account holder name + balances
   → Verify account-holder name matches ID via ER (Chapter 4)
   → Run reconciliation rules (Chapter 10) on structured data
   → Skip statement OCR entirely
3. Else (no Open Banking coverage):
   → Customer uploads PDF statement
   → Parse via OCR pipeline (Chapter 2)
   → Run reconciliation rules on extracted data
   → Surface confidence and audit trail as before
Pattern 2 — Open Banking as cross-check

Both: customer connects via Open Banking and uploads PDF. The two data sources should agree. If they don't (statement shows transactions absent from Open Banking, or vice versa), flag for human review — this is high-signal fraud detection that pure OCR cannot match.

11.7 Re-screening cadence and incremental updates

Lists update daily (sometimes hourly). Re-screening every customer against every list every hour is expensive and unnecessary. The pattern:

  • On list update, the vendor emits a delta (entries added, modified, removed).
  • For each delta, you re-screen candidate customers (those whose names could match using blocking rules — same surname stem, same DOB year).
  • New hits become new ScreeningHit nodes with valid_at = list_update_time.
  • Withdrawn entries cause existing hits to be invalidated: invalid_at = list_update_time, the edge persists for audit.

This is straightforward to build and is what gives you bitemporal-correct "what did we know on date T" replay (Chapter 16).

13.4 The canonical-ID problem — the hardest part

Company canonicalization

"Google" on one CV, "Google LLC" on another, "Alphabet Inc." on a third, "Google DeepMind" on a fourth. These are not the same company in any rigorous sense — but for "who has worked at Google" they need to be linked. Approach:

  1. LEI lookup first. When a CV gives enough info (registered name, country), attempt GLEIF resolution.
  2. OpenCorporates fallback. Jurisdiction-keyed match by registration number or normalized name.
  3. Wikidata Q-ID for well-knowns. "Google" → Q95.
  4. Fuzzy match against your own canonical-company table with Splink, using LEI and OpenCorporates as ground truth where available.
  5. Parent-subsidiary relationships as graph edges, not by collapsing entities. (:Company "Google LLC")-[:SUBSIDIARY_OF]->(:Company "Alphabet Inc."). Queries can choose to traverse or not.

School canonicalization

Easier: ROR (Research Organization Registry) covers ~100k institutions globally with stable IDs. OpenAlex Institution IDs and GRID as backup. For non-research institutions (community colleges, vocational schools), Wikidata or a custom canonical table.

Skill canonicalization

ESCO is the European framework: multilingual labels, hierarchy, ~14,000 skills. O*NET for the US. Lightcast is commercial and broader. For a multilingual platform, start with ESCO — it has the best language coverage and is government-maintained.

13.5 The two queries that justify the whole architecture

Query 1: who worked at Company X in the last 3 years?

MATCH (p:Person)-[w:WORKED_AT]->(c:Company {id: $companyX})
WHERE w.start_valid < datetime()
  AND (w.end_valid IS NULL
       OR w.end_valid > datetime() - duration("P3Y"))
RETURN p, w.role_id AS role,
       w.start_valid AS start, w.end_valid AS end
ORDER BY coalesce(w.end_valid, date("9999-01-01")) DESC

Note the temporal logic: an employment counts if it started before today AND (is currently active OR ended within the last 3 years). The graph-native interval check is trivial. The vector-store version would require post-retrieval date filtering on parsed strings — slower, error-prone, and often missing data.

Query 2: co-worker overlap — who worked together at the same company at the same time?

MATCH (p1:Person)-[w1:WORKED_AT]->(c:Company)<-[w2:WORKED_AT]-(p2:Person)
WHERE p1.id < p2.id  // dedupe undirected pair
  // interval intersection: overlap if start1 < end2 AND start2 < end1
  AND w1.start_valid < coalesce(w2.end_valid, date("9999-01-01"))
  AND w2.start_valid < coalesce(w1.end_valid, date("9999-01-01"))
RETURN p1, p2, c,
       w1.start_valid AS p1_start, w1.end_valid AS p1_end,
       w2.start_valid AS p2_start, w2.end_valid AS p2_end,
       // compute overlap duration
       duration.between(
         apoc.coll.max([w1.start_valid, w2.start_valid]),
         apoc.coll.min([
           coalesce(w1.end_valid, date()),
           coalesce(w2.end_valid, date())
         ])
       ) AS overlap_duration
ORDER BY overlap_duration DESC

Two principles:

  • Both endpoints canonicalized. If "Google LLC" and "Google" are separate nodes, the query fails silently. Canonicalization (Section 13.4) is the binding constraint on correctness.
  • Interval intersection on edge attributes. No vector store can express this.

13.6 Skill expansion — where embeddings help

"Find engineers with Kubernetes experience" needs to also surface engineers with Docker, containerd, Helm, OpenShift. Store an embedding on each :Skill node, build a vector index, and at query time expand the skill set:

MATCH (target:Skill {label: "Kubernetes"})
CALL db.index.vector.queryNodes("skill_embedding", 15, target.embedding)
YIELD node AS similar_skill, score
WHERE score > 0.7
MATCH (p:Person)-[:HAS_SKILL]->(similar_skill)
RETURN DISTINCT p, collect(DISTINCT similar_skill.label) AS skills_matched

The skill embedding can come from a sentence-transformer over ESCO labels + descriptions, or from a pre-trained skill-similarity model. Pre-compute and store; do not recompute at query time.

13.7 Job-trajectory inference

From the same employment edges, derive higher-level features:

  • Tenure distribution per person. Job-hopper vs long-term.
  • Sector-switching pattern. Fintech-only, or fintech → SaaS → AI?
  • Seniority progression. IC → senior IC → manager → director.
  • Company-size trajectory. Startups only, or scaled across small/mid/large?

These are derived nodes or attributes computed offline and refreshed nightly. They become filter conditions on more nuanced queries.

Policy-aware agents over corporate documents

Reading list · ACL patterns ~60 min read

14.1 The hard rule

Non-negotiable

The agent cannot read what the user cannot read. Every retrieval result must be filtered by the user's access rights at the time of the query. Not "the LLM was told not to reveal it" — that is theatre. Filtered at the index, mathematically, before the LLM sees a single token.

14.5 The audit-log discipline

Log every retrieval decision. Not just what was returned, but what was denied: which chunks the user could not see, and why. This is what makes incident response possible — when a leak is suspected, you can re-run the user's queries with their historical access rights and check whether the content actually passed the filter.

This is also the discipline that lets you prove negatives. "Did this user ever see document X?" is a query against the audit log, returning yes/no with timestamps.

14.6 The agent-action problem

A pure search agent only reads. An agent that acts (creates Jira tickets, sends Slack messages, posts to Confluence) needs an authorization layer on every tool invocation. The pattern:

  • Every tool call is authorized by OPA against (user, action, resource, attributes).
  • The agent receives the user's effective permissions, not its own.
  • Actions are logged with the originating user, the agent's intermediate reasoning, and the OPA decision.
  • Destructive actions require explicit user confirmation in the loop.

This is where MCP (Model Context Protocol) is converging — tools as first-class resources with auth boundaries. The PROV-AGENT provenance model (Chapter 17) is designed for exactly this surface.

14.7 The graph version

If your corporate-documents use case has rich structure (project hierarchies, document references, person-page-team relationships), promoting it to a graph buys you the same retrieval advantages as KYC. Cypher queries can express "find the policy document my team owns about X," "find the latest version of the OKRs for project Y that I have access to," "find people who have touched this codebase in the last quarter" — none of which is a clean vector-search task.

The named-graph discipline (Chapter 5) becomes the ACL discipline: every fact is in a named graph keyed by source document; user retrieval intersects allowed source documents.

Chapter 16 · Part V

Bitemporal knowledge — the chapter the evidence supports most strongly

Evidenced ~70 min read

This is the chapter that, more than any other, separates a real audit-grade platform from a clever demo. Bitemporal storage is the single architectural decision that makes "what did we know on date T, and what is true now, and how do those differ" answerable.

16.1 The two times

TimeDefinitionKYC example
Valid timeWhen the fact is true in the real world"Employed at Acme from 2019-01-15 to 2022-06-30"
System time (a.k.a. transaction time)When the system knew the fact"We ingested this CV on 2026-04-12 and corrected the end date on 2026-04-19"

Single-time stores conflate these. Bitemporal stores keep them separately, so every query specifies both. The space of queries becomes two-dimensional: project to "what is true right now," to "what was true at valid_time T," to "what did we believe at system_time T'," or to the genuinely useful "what did we believe at T' about the state at T."

16.2 The motivation, in one scenario

Evidence · the auditor scenario

From the XTDB documentation: "an auditor asks you to justify a decision that you made at time T3 [but] that's not the data you based your decision on." This is the inescapable consequence of single-time storage. By the time the auditor asks, the data has changed — new facts have arrived, errors have been corrected, list versions have updated. Without bitemporal, you literally cannot reconstruct what you knew. With bitemporal, you query at system_time = T3 and get the exact state your decision was based on.

For a KYC platform, this is not a "nice-to-have." It is the difference between defensible decisions and indefensible ones.

16.3 The verified architectural patterns

XTDB — Epochal Time Model

Inspired by Rich Hickey's "Database-as-a-Value" thesis. Every database state is an immutable value; queries are functions of (database, valid_time, system_time). Implementation: every fact stored with a (valid_from, valid_to, system_from, system_to) tuple; insertions append, never overwrite; the index supports both temporal axes.

The killer property: time travel is a primitive, not a feature. Every query naturally projects to the right point in both time axes; no special "history table" or "audit shadow table."

Graphiti — bitemporal edges in a property graph

Every relationship carries:

created_at:  datetime  // when this edge was first written
valid_at:    datetime  // when the fact became true
invalid_at:  datetime  // when the fact ceased to be true
expired_at:  datetime  // when we learned it ceased

The verified architectural difference vs Microsoft GraphRAG: Graphiti "intelligently uses the temporal metadata to update or invalidate, but not discard, outdated information." A new CV that contradicts an old one does not overwrite — it adds a new edge with new validity, and the old edge gets invalid_at set. Both edges remain queryable. The graph is monotonic.

16.4 The implementation patterns, concretely

Pattern 1: SCD Type 2 on lakehouse tables

Slowly-Changing Dimension Type 2 is the lakehouse-native bitemporal pattern:

CREATE TABLE person_state (
  person_id        VARCHAR,
  surname          VARCHAR,
  given_names      VARCHAR,
  nationality      VARCHAR,
  valid_from       DATE,        -- when this fact became true
  valid_to         DATE,        -- when this fact ceased to be true
  system_from      TIMESTAMP,   -- when we learned this
  system_to        TIMESTAMP,   -- when we learned it changed
  source_doc_hash  VARCHAR
);

-- "as of" query: what did we believe on 2026-04-15 about the state on 2024-06-01?
SELECT * FROM person_state
WHERE person_id = 'p:42'
  AND '2024-06-01' BETWEEN valid_from AND valid_to
  AND '2026-04-15' BETWEEN system_from AND system_to;

Two indexed timestamp ranges per row. Insertions append. Updates create new rows and close the old row's valid_to / system_to.

Pattern 2: bitemporal edges in a property graph

The old edge is preserved with end_system set; the new edge is open-ended. Queries at system_time = 2026-04-18 see the old fact; queries at system_time = 2026-04-20 see the new one.

16.5 The four canonical queries

Q1: Current truth (default)

"What is true right now?" — WHERE valid covers now AND system covers now. The default for nearly every UI.

Q2: As-of valid (historical truth)

"What was true on 2022-06-30?" — WHERE 2022-06-30 BETWEEN valid_from AND valid_to AND system covers now. The current best understanding of the past.

Q3: As-of system (audit replay)

"What did we believe on 2026-04-15?" — WHERE valid covers now AND 2026-04-15 BETWEEN system_from AND system_to. The auditor's question.

Q4: Bitemporal (forensic)

"What did we believe on 2026-04-15 about the state on 2022-06-30?" — both bounds. The forensic question: "was the decision we made on 2026-04-15 reasonable given the data we had then?"

Chapter 17 · Part V

Provenance, citations, and auditability

Partly evidenced ~55 min read

Provenance is the discipline of recording the origin and transformation history of every fact in your system. For regulated domains, it is the deliverable. For agentic workflows, it is what makes the agent's output trustworthy. For your platform, it is what lets you replay history and explain decisions.

17.1 The W3C PROV foundation

W3C PROV is the international standard for provenance. Three core concepts:

  • Entity — a thing whose provenance we want to track (a document, an extracted fact, a decision).
  • Activity — a process that uses and produces entities (an extraction run, an ER computation, a screening call).
  • Agent — who or what is responsible (a user, a service, a model).

Relationships: Activity wasAssociatedWith Agent, Activity used Entity, Activity generated Entity, Entity wasDerivedFrom Entity, Entity wasAttributedTo Agent. Together they form a provenance graph parallel to your data graph.

17.2 PROV-AGENT — the verified extension for agentic workflows

Evidence

arXiv 2508.02866 introduces PROV-AGENT: "a provenance model that extends W3C PROV and leverages the Model Context Protocol (MCP) and data observability to integrate agent interactions into end-to-end workflow provenance." Includes an open-source near-real-time capture system and evaluation across edge / cloud / HPC.

Important refutation: The sub-claim that PROV-AGENT operates at span-level granularity was refuted (0-3) in adversarial verification. PROV-AGENT covers workflow-level provenance for agent interactions. Span-level provenance (chunk → page → bbox) must be built on top.

17.3 The architectural pattern, end-to-end

  1. Content-addressable storage — every source byte stream is stored under sha256(bytes). Documents are immutable. Once ingested, they cannot be retroactively altered.
  2. Signed extraction artifacts — every extraction emits {source_hash, extractor_version, prompt_hash, schema_hash, output, signature} signed with a service key. Tamper-evident.
  3. Span-level citations on every extracted field — {field_value, page, bbox, ocr_text, ocr_confidence, extractor_model, extractor_version}.
  4. PROV-AGENT events at workflow boundaries — Activity(extract_id) used Entity(source_hash); wasAssociatedWith Agent(extractor_model_v2); generated Entity(extraction_hash).
  5. ER provenance — every RESOLVED_TO edge carries the model version, the feature breakdown, and the threshold in effect at decision time.
  6. Decision provenance — every case decision carries the rule versions that fired, the LLM-judge calls (with prompts), the human reviewer (if any).
  7. "Show your work" UX — every number on every screen clicks through to its source.

17.4 The event schema you ship

        "document_ingested",

Emitted as an OpenLineage-compatible event into a durable log (Kafka, Kinesis, EventHubs). Replayable into the lakehouse. Auditable.

17.5 Span-level citations — building on top of PROV-AGENT

The discipline: every extracted field carries its span from extraction onward. The span is part of the data; it is never an afterthought. UI components render fields as clickable cards:

The UI primitive is a FieldValue render. Behind it is the bbox, the source document hash, the extractor version, the OCR confidence. The analyst's experience is "click any number, see the source." The regulator's experience is the same.

17.6 The replay capability

Given the discipline above, you can answer:

  • "Re-run case C with the rule version from 2026-01" — load the rule, replay the decision, diff the outcome.
  • "What would we extract from document D under our new extractor?" — re-extract, compare to the original extraction artifact, surface differences.
  • "Which past decisions would change under the current sanctions list?" — re-screen the entire historical population, compare to original.

Replay is what turns an audit-grade platform into a quality-management platform. It is also what surfaces drift: extractor accuracy degrades, ER calibration shifts, rule efficacy changes. Without replay, you cannot measure these.

17.7 The trust model

Signed artifacts and content-addressable storage are not theatre. They are what lets you say, in the face of an inspector:

  • "This is the exact byte sequence the customer submitted."
  • "This is the exact extraction we produced; signed; cannot have been altered."
  • "This is the exact rule version in effect at the time."
  • "Here is the OpenLineage / PROV-AGENT trail."

The platform's claim is not "we made the right decision." It is "here is everything we knew, when, how, and what we decided based on it." That claim is much stronger because it is verifiable.

18.2 The gold-set discipline

The most important investment in your eval program is a frozen test set of 500–1000 real cases, hand-labeled by your most senior analysts, covering your full document distribution (jurisdictions, languages, image quality, edge cases). Every model, rule, or extractor change is regressed against this set before deployment.

Properties of a good gold set:

  • Real, not synthetic. Synthetic data misses the long tail of real-world quirks.
  • Stratified. Reflects your customer mix; not biased toward the easy cases.
  • Includes negatives. Forged documents, inconsistent submissions, cases that should be rejected.
  • Versioned. When you add cases, version the set; report metrics against named versions.
  • Locked. The set is never modified to make a regression "pass." If a case is wrong-labeled, you correct it and document the change.

18.3 Calibration plots — the ER-specific eval

Plot predicted posterior probability vs observed match rate, binned by 5% buckets. A well-calibrated ER model produces a 45° diagonal: pairs predicted at 0.9 actually match 90% of the time. Deviations tell you where to retune.

This is the eval that catches population shifts: if the model was trained on EU customers and is being applied to LATAM, the calibration plot will warp before any obvious metric moves.

  • Path correctness: for multi-hop queries, was the path the LLM cited actually present in the graph?
  • Temporal correctness: for time-bounded queries, were retrieved facts valid at the asked time?
  • Provenance integrity: does every claim in the answer trace back to a real source?

Build custom metrics for these alongside the off-the-shelf RAG metrics.

18.6 Red-team discipline

Monthly, generate adversarial documents and measure extractor / ER / validation behavior:

  • Name mutations: transliteration variants, deliberate typos, married-name updates, double-surname collapses.
  • Date ambiguity: dates in unusual formats, ambiguous DD/MM vs MM/DD.
  • Forged-style features: deliberately inconsistent MRZ + VIZ, doctored signatures, mismatched fonts.
  • Document-class confusion: a residence permit submitted where a passport is expected; an old expired ID; a printed photocopy of a screen.

Track per-attack class accuracy over time. The trend is what matters; a single number is noise.

18.7 Drift monitoring

Set up dashboards that watch:

  • Extraction field-level F1 per document type per month.
  • ER posterior distribution histogram — shifts indicate population change.
  • Retrieval recall@10 on a held-out probe set, run weekly.
  • Human-override rate by week — increasing means thresholds need recalibration.

Drift is the silent killer. The model that worked great in pilot quietly degrades over six months as the customer mix shifts. Dashboards are the only defense.

Frontier and research directions

Reading list · watch items ~40 min read

The field is moving. Five threads to track that will materially affect what you build over the next 24 months.

20.1 Neuro-symbolic agents

LLMs that emit programs over symbolic knowledge graphs rather than free-form text. The agent's output is not a paragraph; it is a Cypher query, an OPA policy, a Pydantic-typed record. The compiler metaphor is exactly the trust shape regulators want.

20.3 Structured world models

The research direction: LLMs that maintain explicit belief states over a structured world (graph or relational), update them under new evidence, and reason against them with both natural-language and symbolic ops. For long-running agents (an analyst's assistant that follows a case for weeks), this is the missing piece. Most current "agent memory" is a thin retrieval layer; a real world model has consistency invariants, conflict resolution, and entailment.

20.4 "Schema-first" agents

The discipline of giving the LLM the schema, not the data. The LLM writes a query against the schema; the runtime executes the query and returns typed results; the agent reasons over the typed results. This is dramatically more robust than retrieve-and-stuff RAG and is the right pattern for any enterprise agent over a real schema.

Concrete examples: Glean's enterprise search emitting structured queries; LangGraph's typed nodes; Cohere North's structured retrieval; Anthropic tool-use with strict schemas; the broader "function-calling first" movement in 2025–2026.

Open questions worth investigating before commitment

Due-diligence backlog ~10 min read

Treat each of these as a 1–4 week investigation before you lock the corresponding architectural decision.

Q1 · Graphiti reproducibility at your scale

Can Graphiti's claimed 300ms P95 retrieval latency be reproduced on your own KYC corpus at 10M+ bitemporal edges? How does it compare against equivalent implementations on KuzuDB, FalkorDB, Memgraph, or Neo4j-LangChain GraphRAG with the same bitemporal schema?

Investigation plan: build a synthetic 10M-edge bitemporal graph; implement the same retrieval logic against four candidate stores; measure P50/P95/P99 for representative query patterns (1-hop, 3-hop, temporal, multi-hop).

Q2 · Extraction-quality ceiling on your real documents

What is the field-level F1 ceiling for XGrammar + Pydantic + dual-LLM verifier on your specific document classes — passports/IDs with MRZ+PDF417, bank statements with line items, business registrations across jurisdictions, signature/stamp detection? Where does the marginal engineering dollar buy more accuracy: better OCR (Textract/Azure DI/Mistral OCR/Docling), better extraction grammar, or better post-hoc validation rules?

Investigation plan: pick 200 real documents covering your distribution; hand-label a gold set; run the extraction pipeline; measure per-field F1; ablate one component at a time (swap OCR provider; remove grammar constraints; remove dual-verifier) and measure delta.

Q3 · ER calibration transfer across populations

How well do Fellegi–Sunter m and u parameters trained on one population (e.g., EU customers) transfer to another (LATAM, MENA) given known issues with name transliteration, address normalization, and cultural name structure? At what population shift does retraining beat hand-tuned blocking rules plus active-learning (Zingg-style) approaches?

Investigation plan: train Splink on a synthetic EU population; evaluate on a synthetic MENA population; measure precision/recall drop; retrain; measure recovery; compare to Zingg starting from scratch on MENA.

Q4 · Policy-aware retrieval — pick the canonical pattern

For the corporate-documents use case, what is the empirically correct architecture for enforcing "agent cannot read what user cannot read" across a bitemporal KG — OPA/Rego over the retrieval result set, attribute-based access control baked into named-graph provenance, row/column ACLs propagated from the source system, or hybrid? Which production system (Glean, Onyx/Danswer, Vectara, Writer) has the battle-tested implementation worth copying versus reinventing?

Investigation plan: read Glean and Onyx public architecture writeups; build a minimal prototype with each ACL pattern over the same dataset; red-team with privilege-escalation attempts; measure latency overhead per pattern.

Q5 · Where do agents fail in your specific workflows?

Beyond infrastructure: which KYC decisions are actually hard for an agent to make correctly, and what is the human-in-loop interaction pattern that maximizes throughput without sacrificing audit quality?

Investigation plan: ride along with analysts at a friendly customer for one week; log every decision they make and what evidence they consulted; identify the 5 hardest decision classes; design agent + human workflows for each.
