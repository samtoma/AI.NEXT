What to look for

Structural validation already passed: every tier is one the selector can ask for, every multiple-choice key appears among its own options, no two options share text, and every item has a worked solution. None of that is a check on the mathematics.

The defects worth your time are the ones a machine cannot see. An answer key that disagrees with its own worked solution. A distractor that is also correct. A stem that is ambiguous rather than wrong. A step that reaches the right answer by faulty reasoning — which matters more here than usual, because the worked solution is exactly what the tutor teaches from when a student misses the question.

Each distractor claims to encode a named misconception. That claim is load-bearing: it is what lets a wrong answer pull the matching refutation from the explanation library without a classifier. If a distractor does not really represent the error it names, the student gets an explanation for a mistake they did not make.

What happens to your verdicts

Accept, Reject and Needs a fix are saved as you click. A rejection carries further than the single item: each generated question names the reviewed book question it was derived from, so a defect can be traced to its family and that family retired together rather than the whole bank being distrusted.

What to do about a rejected family is not settled yet — it is the open question in ADR-0008, and the first rejection is what should settle it.
