conceptual mc:g10m8s3-2-1:negative-coordinate-subtraction-slip

Loses a sign subtracting negative coordinates

When working out y2-y1 or x2-x1 and one of the values is already negative, the student simplifies a-(-b) as a-b instead of a+b.

Refutation
  1. You know how to subtract two coordinates — the trouble starts only when one of them is already negative, and the two minus signs sitting together get collapsed into one instead of cancelling to a plus.
  2. The book's own working for B(-6,-3) and C(-2,3) writes the gradient as mBC=(3-(-3))/(-2-(-6)) and turns it into (3+3)/(-2+6)=6/4=3/2 — every -(- ) becomes a + before anything else happens.
  3. Skip that step instead and read 3-(-3) as 3-3 and -2-(-6) as -2-6: you'd get 0/-8=0 — a horizontal line, nowhere near the book's y=3/2x+6.
  4. Whenever you see two minus signs next to each other, rewrite a-(-b) as a+b first, before you simplify anything else.
generated_distractor mc:g10m8s3-2-1:swaps-coordinates-when-substituting

Swaps which number is x and which is y

The student substitutes a point's two values into the wrong slots — putting the y-value where x belongs, or the reverse — when solving for c in y=mx+c or for a missing coordinate.

Signal: The reported value equals what you'd get by putting the point's coordinates in the opposite slots — e.g. a constant of -1 instead of the book's 5/2 for a line through P(-3,4).

Refutation
  1. You've got the right idea — substitute a known point into the equation — but the two numbers from the point have gone into the wrong places: the y-value where x should be, or the other way round.
  2. This objective's question on PQ finds m=-\tfrac12 and uses point P(-3,4). The book substitutes x=-3, y=4: 4=-\tfrac12(-3)+c, so c=5/2, giving y=-\tfrac12x+\tfrac52.
  3. Swap the two numbers instead — put x=4, y=-3 — and you'd get -3=-\tfrac12(4)+c, so c=-1: a completely different line, y=-\tfrac12x-1, that doesn't even pass through P.
  4. The same mix-up shows up the other way in the 'find the missing coordinate' questions: the diagram for point B(2, a) tells you x=2, so substitute it for x in the equation and solve for y — never plug the labelled number in for y just because it's the one you're given first.
book_distractor mc:g10m8s3-2-2:stops-at-partial-quadrilateral-property

Names the shape from only one of the properties that build up to it

The student correctly checks one defining property of the quadrilateral (one pair of sides parallel, one pair of sides equal in length, or one right angle / one perpendicular or equal pair of diagonals) but doesn't go on to check the other property the book combines with it, so answers with the less-specific relative that matches only what was checked.

Signal: final answer names a related shape (parallelogram, trapezium, rhombus, rectangle or kite) that the working partly supports, rather than the single most specific shape (isosceles trapezium or square) the full check gives.

Refutation
  1. They did do a real, correct check — finding one pair of sides parallel, or the diagonals perpendicular, or one right angle — and that check is a genuine, useful piece of the answer, just not the whole of it.
  2. For the quadrilateral with A(0, 3), B(4, 3), C(5, -1) and D(-1, -1), AB∥ DC (both gradient 0) is true and is the book's first fact — but the book then also compares the lengths of the other pair of opposite sides, AD and BC, and finds them equal; only that second check earns 'isosceles.' Stopping after the parallel check alone gives 'trapezium'; assuming the other pair must also be parallel because it's equal in length gives 'parallelogram' — but mAD=4 and mBC=-4 are not equal, so that pair is not parallel.
  3. For the parallelogram LMNP with L(-1, -1), M(-2, 4) and P(4, 0), showing mMP× mLN=-1 (diagonals perpendicular) proves LMNP is a rhombus — true, but only half the picture; it takes a right angle as well, LM⊥ MN, to earn 'square'. A square is also a rectangle, a rhombus and a kite, so those names are true, just less precise — not wrong, just not the finished answer.
  4. Once one property is confirmed, ask what shape it proves on its own — then go back and check the book's other property (the missing side-length, angle, or diagonal fact) before naming the quadrilateral, so the answer is the single most specific shape both facts together support.
